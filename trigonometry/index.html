Introduction

In this course, we will explore trigonometric values of angles and several related concepts (how to calculate them, identities, conversions, etc.).

We will also cover the main trigonometric functions, key types of trigonometric equations, and several useful formulas.

Before jumping into those, let's review a few basic concepts we'll need.

Basic Concepts

Right Triangles

A right triangle is a triangle that has one right angle, that is, an angle equal to \(90^{\circ}.\)

All right triangles have:

  • One right angle \((90^{\circ}).\)
  • Two acute angles \((\lt 90^{\circ}).\)
  • Two legs (the sides adjacent to the right angle).
  • One hypotenuse (the side opposite the right angle (and also the longest side of the triangle)).

For example:

In this triangle:

  • Angle \(\hat{B}\) is the right angle.
  • Angles \(\hat{A}\) and \(\hat{C}\) are acute angles.
  • Sides \(AB\) and \(BC\) are the legs.
  • Side \(AC\) is the hypotenuse.

Also, by the Pythagorean Theorem, \(AB^2+BC^2=AC^2,\) where \(AB, BC,\) and \(AC\) represent the lengths of the respective sides.

Trigonometric Ratios Of Acute Angles

For an acute angle \(ω\) in a right triangle, we define the trigonometric ratios as follows:

  • \(\sin(ω)=\) \(\cfrac{\text{opposite leg}}{\text{hypotenuse}}\)
  • \(\cos(ω)=\) \(\cfrac{\text{adjacent leg}}{\text{hypotenuse}}\)
  • \(\tan(ω)=\) \(\cfrac{\text{opposite leg}}{\text{adjacent leg}}\)
  • \(\csc(ω)=\) \(\cfrac{\text{hypotenuse}}{\text{opposite leg}}\)
  • \(\sec(ω)=\) \(\cfrac{\text{hypotenuse}}{\text{adjacent leg}}\)
  • \(\cot(ω)=\) \(\cfrac{\text{adjacent leg}}{\text{opposite leg}}\)

Notes

  • \(ω\) is read as "omega".
  • \(\sin(ω)\) is read as "sine of \(ω\)".
  • \(\cos(ω)\) is read as "cosine of \(ω\)".
  • \(\tan(ω)\) is read as "tangent of \(ω\)".
  • \(\sec(ω)\) is read as "secant of \(ω\)".
  • \(\csc(ω)\) is read as "cosecant of \(ω\)".
  • \(\cot(ω)\) is read as "cotangent of \(ω\)".
  • By "opposite leg", we mean the leg opposite angle \(ω.\)
  • By "adjacent leg", we mean the leg next to the angle \(ω\) (not the hypotenuse).
  • Additionally, whenever we refer to a side (leg or hypotenuse), we mean the length of that specific side.

Therefore:

  • \(\sin(ω)=\cfrac{AB}{AC}=\cfrac{4}{5}\)
  • \(\cos(ω)=\cfrac{BC}{AC}=\cfrac{3}{5}\)
  • \(\tan(ω)=\cfrac{AB}{BC}=\cfrac{4}{3}\)
  • \(\csc(ω)=\cfrac{AC}{AB}=\cfrac{5}{4}\)
  • \(\sec(ω)=\cfrac{AC}{BC}=\cfrac{5}{3}\)

  • \(\sin(θ)=\cfrac{BC}{AC}=\cfrac{3}{5}\)

  • \(\tan(θ)=\cfrac{BC}{AB}=\cfrac{3}{4}\)
  • \(\csc(θ)=\cfrac{AC}{BC}=\cfrac{5}{3}\)

  • \(\cot(θ)=\cfrac{AB}{BC}=\cfrac{4}{3}\)

Important Note

From these definitions, we get four fundamental relationships:

  • \(\sec(ω)=\cfrac{1}{\cos(ω)}\)
  • \(\csc(ω)=\cfrac{1}{\sin(ω)}\)
  • \(\tan(ω)=\cfrac{\sin(ω)}{\cos(ω)}\)
  • \(\cot(ω)=\cfrac{1}{\tan(ω)}=\cfrac{\cos(ω)}{\sin(ω)}\)

These are crucial because they mean if we know the sine and cosine of an angle, we automatically know all other trigonometric ratios as well.

Also, since secant, cosecant, and cotangent are just the reciprocals of cosine, sine, and tangent respectively, we usually focus more on sine, cosine, and tangent in trigonometry.

Finally, for the trigonometric values of other angles (negative angles, \(0^{\circ},\) \(90^{\circ},\) and angles greater than \(90^{\circ}\)), we evaluate them using the unit circle, which we'll cover shortly.

Circles

Every circle has two fundamental elements:

  • Its center: the point equidistant from every point on the circle.
  • Its radius: the distance from the center to any point on the circle.

Note that a circle refers only to the boundary curve itself, not its interior.

Arcs

An arc is any continuous segment of a circle.

Examples of arcs include semicircles, quarter-circles, or any connected segment of a circle.

An arc can be defined in two ways:

  • By its length: the length along the curve of the arc.
  • By its central angle: the angle formed by two radii connecting the endpoints of the arc to the circle's center.

Radians

We usually measure angles in degrees, but in mathematics another unit of measurement is used very often: radians.

A radian is defined as the central angle that corresponds to an arc with a length equal to the radius of the circle.

You probably didn't understand exactly what this means, but that's okay.

If you think about it, you probably don't know exactly what degrees are either. You simply know that \(360^{\circ}\) correspond to a full rotation, and so you understand what angles \(180^{\circ},\) \(90^{\circ},\) \(45^{\circ},\) etc, correspond to.

The same is enough for radians.

The only things you need to know are that:

  • Radians are another unit for measuring angles.
  • They are represented as "rad", from the word "radians".
  • \(360^{\circ},\) which correspond to one full circle, are equal to \(2π\) rad.

Based on this:

  • \(180^{\circ}\) (half a circle) are equal to \(π\) rad.
  • \(90^{\circ}\) are equal to \(\cfrac{π}{2}\) rad.
  • \(45^{\circ}\) are equal to \(\cfrac{π}{4}\) rad.
  • \(0^{\circ}\) are equal to \(0\) rad.
  • Etc.

In general, to convert any angle from degrees to radians and vice versa, we use the formula \(\cfrac{a}{π}=\cfrac{d}{180},\) where \(a\) is the angle in radians and \(d\) is the same angle in degrees.

By substituting the angle written in the unit of measurement we know, the only unknown left is the angle in the other unit of measurement.

Example

Let's convert \(12^{\circ}\) into radians:

\(\quad ! \quad \cfrac{a}{π}=\cfrac{d}{180}\iff \cfrac{a}{π}=\cfrac{12}{180}\iff \cfrac{a}{π}=\cfrac{1}{15}\iff a=\cfrac{π}{15}\) rad

Now, let's convert \(\cfrac{π}{6}\) rad into degrees:

\(\quad ! \quad \cfrac{a}{π}=\cfrac{d}{180}\iff \cfrac{\frac{π}{6}}{π}=\cfrac{d}{180}\iff \cfrac{1}{6}=\cfrac{d}{180}\iff d=\cfrac{180}{6}\iff d=30^{\circ}\)

The Unit Circle

From here on, all angles mentioned will be in radians (rad) unless specified otherwise in degrees.

Trigonometric Values Of Any Angle

In a Cartesian coordinate system (i.e., the Cartesian plane), we draw a circle centered at \((0,0)\) with a radius of \(1.\)

Using this circle, known as the unit circle, we can calculate the trigonometric values of any angle as follows:

  • Suppose we want to find the trigonometric values of an angle \(ω.\)
  • We start with a line segment connecting \((0,0)\) to \((1,0).\) This segment acts as a radius of the circle.
  • Keeping its fixed endpoint at \((0,0),\) we rotate the segment counterclockwise until it forms the desired angle \(ω\) with the positive \(x\)-axis.
  • Rotating the segment simply means moving its endpoint from \((1,0)\) along the circle to a new point that defines angle \(ω\) relative to the positive \(x\)-axis.
  • Once positioned, this moving endpoint lands on a point on the circle with coordinates \((x,y).\)
  • We then define:
    • \(\sin(ω)=y\)
    • \(\cos(ω)=x\)
    • \(\tan(ω)=\cfrac{y}{x},\) for \(x\neq 0\)
    • \(\csc(ω)=\cfrac{1}{y},\) for \(y\neq 0\)
    • \(\sec(ω)=\cfrac{1}{x},\) for \(x\neq 0\)
    • \(\cot(ω)=\cfrac{x}{y},\) for \(y\neq 0\)

As you can see, the familiar relationships \(\tan(ω)=\cfrac{\sin(ω)}{\cos(ω)},\) \(\csc(ω)=\cfrac{1}{\sin(ω)},\) \(\sec(ω)=\cfrac{1}{\cos(ω)},\) and \(\cot(ω)=\cfrac{1}{\tan(ω)}=\cfrac{\cos(ω)}{\sin(ω)}\) from right-triangle trigonometry hold true here as well.

Additionally, we can visualize \(\tan(ω)\) geometrically:

  • Extend the line segment until it intersects the vertical line \(x=1.\) The \(y\)-coordinate of this intersection point equals \(\tan(ω).\)
    Notice that if the line segment is vertical—meaning its terminal point lies at \((0,1)\) or \((0,-1)\)—extending it will never intersect the line \(x=1.\) For this reason, \(\tan(\frac{π}{2})\) and \(\tan(\frac{3π}{2})\) are undefined.
  • We can find \(\sec(ω),\) \(\csc(ω),\) and \(\cot(ω)\) geometrically too, but since they are just the reciprocals of \(\cos(ω),\) \(\sin(ω),\) and \(\tan(ω)\) respectively, we don't really need to.
    It's enough to find \(\sin(ω)\) and \(\cos(ω).\)

To form an angle of \(0,\) we do not rotate the segment at all.

To form an angle of \(\cfrac{π}{2},\) we rotate the segment counterclockwise until its terminal point reaches \((0,1):\)

Therefore:

  • \(\csc(\frac{π}{2})=\cfrac{1}{1}=1\)
  • \(\sec(\frac{π}{2}):\) undefined
  • \(\cot(\frac{π}{2})=\cfrac{0}{1}=0\)

To form an angle of \(π,\) we rotate the segment until its terminal point reaches \((-1,0):\)

Therefore:

  • \(\sin(π)=0\)
  • \(\cos(π)=-1\)

  • \(\csc(π):\) undefined

Example \(4\)

Let's find the trigonometric values for \(\cfrac{3π}{2}\) \((270^{\circ}).\)

To form an angle of \(\cfrac{3π}{2},\) we rotate the segment until its terminal point reaches \((0,-1):\)

Therefore:

  • \(\sin(\frac{3π}{2})=-1\)
  • \(\cos(\frac{3π}{2})=0\)
  • \(\tan(\frac{3π}{2}):\) undefined
  • \(\csc(\frac{3π}{2})=\cfrac{1}{-1}=-1\)
  • \(\sec(\frac{3π}{2}):\) undefined
  • \(\cot(\frac{3π}{2})=\cfrac{0}{-1}=0\)

Important: Angles Greater Than \(2π\)

Let's find the trigonometric values for \(2π\) \((360^{\circ}).\)

To form an angle of \(2π,\) we rotate the segment through a complete revolution back to its starting position at \((1,0):\)

Therefore:

  • \(\sin(2π)=0\)
  • \(\cos(2π)=1\)
  • \(\tan(2π)=\cfrac{0}{1}=0\)
  • \(\csc(2π):\) undefined
  • \(\sec(2π)=\cfrac{1}{1}=1\)
  • \(\cot(2π):\) undefined

Because the segment finishes in the exact same position as it does for \(0\) rad, the trigonometric values of both angles (\(0\) and \(2π\)) are identical.

More generally, angles greater than \(2π\) share the exact same trigonometric values as an equivalent angle less than \(2π,\) because each full rotation returns the terminal side to its starting point.

For example, the trigonometric values for \(\cfrac{7π}{2},\) which can be rewritten as \(2π+\cfrac{3π}{2},\) are identical to those for \(\cfrac{3π}{2}.\)

Conclusion

For any angle \(0\le ω \lt 2π\) and \(k\in\mathbb{N}^*,\) we have:

  • \(\sin(k\cdot 2π+ω)=\sin(ω)\)
  • \(\cos(k\cdot 2π+ω)=\cos(ω)\)
  • \(\tan(k\cdot 2π+ω)=\tan(ω)\)
  • \(\csc(k\cdot 2π+ω)=\csc(ω)\)
  • \(\sec(k\cdot 2π+ω)=\sec(ω)\)
  • \(\cot(k\cdot 2π+ω)=\cot(ω)\)

Here, \(k\) represents the number of full revolutions around the circle.

Note

If measuring angles in degrees instead of radians, the formulas above would use \(k\cdot 360^{\circ}\) instead of \(k\cdot 2π.\)

Important: Negative Angles

A negative angle simply means rotating the line segment clockwise rather than counterclockwise.

Naturally, any position reached via a clockwise (negative) rotation can also be reached via a corresponding counterclockwise (positive) rotation.

For example, rotating by \(-\cfrac{π}{2}\) places the terminal point on the circle at \((0,-1).\)

This is the exact same position reached by rotating positive \(\cfrac{3π}{2}:\)

Generally, for any angle \(-ω\) with \(0\lt ω\lt 2π,\) the terminal side lands at the same position as it would for the positive angle \(2π-ω.\)

Conclusion

If \(0\le ω \lt 2π,\) then:

  • \(\sin(-ω)=\sin(2π-ω)\)
  • \(\cos(-ω)=\cos(2π-ω)\)
  • \(\tan(-ω)=\tan(2π-ω)\)
  • \(\csc(-ω)=\csc(2π-ω)\)
  • \(\sec(-ω)=\sec(2π-ω)\)
  • \(\cot(-ω)=\cot(2π-ω)\)

As before, we can also complete multiple full rotations clockwise (making the angle less than \(-2π\)).

These full rotations do not alter the final position, as each revolution returns the segment to where it started.

Conclusion

For any angle \(ω\) in radians and any integer \(k\in\mathbb{Z},\) we have:

  • \(\sin(k\cdot 2π+ω)=\sin(ω)\)
  • \(\cos(k\cdot 2π+ω)=\cos(ω)\)
  • \(\tan(k\cdot 2π+ω)=\tan(ω)\)
  • \(\csc(k\cdot 2π+ω)=\csc(ω)\)
  • \(\sec(k\cdot 2π+ω)=\sec(ω)\)
  • \(\cot(k\cdot 2π+ω)=\cot(ω)\)
Note

In degrees, the term \(k\cdot 2π\) is replaced by \(k\cdot 360^{\circ}.\)

Examples

\(\quad ! \quad \sin(5π)=\sin(2\cdot 2π+π)=\sin(π)=0\)

\(\quad ! \quad \cos(\frac{123π}{6})=\cos(10\cdot 2π+\frac{3π}{6})=\cos(\frac{3π}{6})=\cos(\frac{π}{2})=0\)

\(\quad ! \quad \tan(-\frac{7π}{4})=\tan(2π-\frac{7π}{4})=\tan(\frac{π}{4})=1\)

\(\quad ! \quad \cot(-10π)=\cot(-5\cdot 2π+0)=\cot(0):\) undefined

\(\quad ! \quad \sin(-732^{\circ})=\sin(-2\cdot 360^{\circ}-12^{\circ})=\sin(-12^{\circ})=\sin(360^{\circ}-12^{\circ})=\sin(348^{\circ})\)

\(\quad ! \quad \cos(1080^{\circ})=\cos(3\cdot 360^{\circ}+0)=\cos(0^{\circ})=1\)

\(\quad ! \quad \tan(-990^{\circ})=\tan(-2\cdot 360^{\circ}-270^{\circ})=\tan(-270^{\circ})=\tan(360^{\circ}-270^{\circ})=\tan(90^{\circ}):\) undefined

\(\quad ! \quad \cot(405^{\circ})=\cot(360^{\circ}+45^{\circ})=\cot(45^{\circ})=1\)

Range Of Trigonometric Values

As we've seen, trigonometric values correspond directly to coordinates on the unit circle.

Specifically:

  • Sine takes its values from the \(y\)-axis, ranging from \(-1\) to \(1.\) Thus, for any angle \(ω,\) we have \(-1\le \sin(ω)\le 1.\)
  • Cosine takes its values from the \(x\)-axis, ranging from \(-1\) to \(1.\) Thus, for any angle \(ω,\) we have \(-1\le \cos(ω)\le 1.\)
  • Tangent takes its values as \(y\)-coordinates along the vertical line \(x=1,\) spanning all real numbers. Thus, for any angle \(ω\) for which it is defined, we have \(-\infty\lt\tan(ω)\lt +\infty.\)
  • Cosecant is the reciprocal of sine, for which it holds that \(-1\le \sin(ω)\le 1.\) Therefore, for any angle \(ω,\) we have \(\csc(ω)\le -1\) or \(\csc(ω)\ge 1.\)
  • Secant is the reciprocal of cosine, for which it holds that \(-1\le \cos(ω)\le 1.\) Therefore, for any angle \(ω,\) we have \(\sec(ω)\le -1\) or \(\sec(ω)\ge 1.\)
  • Cotangent is the reciprocal of tangent, for which it holds that \(-\infty\lt \tan(ω)\lt +\infty.\) Therefore, for any angle \(ω\) for which it is defined, we have \(-\infty\lt\cot(ω)\lt +\infty.\)

The inequalities \(-1\le \sin(ω)\le 1\) and \(-1\le \cos(ω)\le 1\) are particularly important and will be used throughout this course.

Note

Using absolute value notation, these inequalities can also be written as:

  • \(|\sin(ω)|\le 1,\) for any angle \(ω\)
  • \(|\cos(ω)|\le 1,\) for any angle \(ω\)

Signs Of Trigonometric Values

By using the unit circle, we can easily determine whether a trigonometric value will be positive or negative for a given angle without explicitly evaluating it.

Specifically, rotating a segment through an angle \(ω\) positions its terminal point at \((x,y)\) on the unit circle.

The coordinates \(x,y\) give us:

  • \(\sin(ω)=y\)
  • \(\cos(ω)=x\)
  • \(\tan(ω)=\cfrac{y}{x},\) for \(x\neq 0\)
  • \(\csc(ω)=\cfrac{1}{y},\) for \(y\neq 0\)
  • \(\sec(ω)=\cfrac{1}{x},\) for \(x\neq 0\)
  • \(\cot(ω)=\cfrac{x}{y},\) for \(y\neq 0\)

Recall the sign conventions for points \((x,y)\) in the Cartesian plane:

  • \(x\gt 0\) and \(y\gt 0\) in Quadrant I.
  • \(x\lt 0\) and \(y\gt 0\) in Quadrant II.
  • \(x\lt 0\) and \(y\lt 0\) in Quadrant III.
  • \(x\gt 0\) and \(y\lt 0\) in Quadrant IV.

From these, we obtain the sign table:

\(ω\)\(0\lt ω\lt \cfrac{π}{2}\)\(\cfrac{π}{2}\lt ω\lt π\)\(π\lt ω\lt \cfrac{3π}{2}\)\(\cfrac{3π}{2}\lt ω\lt 2π\)
\(\sin(ω)\)\(+\)\(+\)\(-\)\(-\)
\(\cos(ω)\)\(+\)\(-\)\(-\)\(+\)
\(\tan(ω)\)\(+\)\(-\)\(+\)\(-\)
\(\csc(ω)\)\(+\)\(+\)\(-\)\(-\)
\(\sec(ω)\)\(+\)\(-\)\(-\)\(+\)
\(\cot(ω)\)\(+\)\(-\)\(+\)\(-\)

In summary:

  • In Quadrant I, all trigonometric values are positive.
  • In Quadrant II, only sine and cosecant are positive.
  • In Quadrant III, only tangent and cotangent are positive.
  • In Quadrant IV, only cosine and secant are positive.

  • \(\csc(\frac{2π}{3})\gt 0\)
  • \(\sec(\frac{2π}{3})\lt 0\)
  • \(\cot(\frac{2π}{3})\lt 0\)

The angle \(\cfrac{5π}{4}\) lies in Quadrant III, so:

  • \(\sin(\frac{5π}{4})\lt 0\)
  • \(\cos(\frac{5π}{4})\lt 0\)
  • \(\tan(\frac{5π}{4})\gt 0\)
  • \(\csc(\frac{5π}{4})\lt 0\)
  • \(\sec(\frac{5π}{4})\lt 0\)
  • \(\cot(\frac{5π}{4})\gt 0\)

The angle \(\cfrac{7π}{4}\) lies in Quadrant IV, so:

  • \(\sin(\frac{7π}{4})\lt 0\)
  • \(\cos(\frac{7π}{4})\gt 0\)
  • \(\tan(\frac{7π}{4})\lt 0\)
  • \(\csc(\frac{7π}{4})\lt 0\)
  • \(\sec(\frac{7π}{4})\gt 0\)
  • \(\cot(\frac{7π}{4})\lt 0\)

Note

If an angle \(ω\) is greater than \(2π,\) we determine its quadrant by finding its equivalent angle after subtracting full revolutions.

For instance, \(\cfrac{9π}{4}\) equals \(2π+\cfrac{π}{4},\) making it equivalent with \(\cfrac{π}{4},\) which lies in Quadrant I.

Therefore, \(\cfrac{9π}{4}\) lies in Quadrant I as well.

Thus:

  • \(\sin(\frac{9π}{4})\gt 0\)
  • \(\cos(\frac{9π}{4})\gt 0\)
  • \(\tan(\frac{9π}{4})\gt 0\)
  • \(\csc(\frac{9π}{4})\gt 0\)
  • \(\sec(\frac{9π}{4})\gt 0\)
  • \(\cot(\frac{9π}{4})\gt 0\)

Similarly, if an angle is negative, we look at its positive equivalent angle.

For example, \(-\cfrac{3π}{5}\) is equivalent with \(2π-\cfrac{3π}{5}=\cfrac{7π}{5},\) which lies in Quadrant III.

Thus:

  • \(\sin(-\frac{3π}{5})\lt 0\)
  • \(\cos(-\frac{3π}{5})\lt 0\)
  • \(\tan(-\frac{3π}{5})\gt 0\)
  • \(\csc(-\frac{3π}{5})\lt 0\)
  • \(\sec(-\frac{3π}{5})\lt 0\)
  • \(\cot(-\frac{3π}{5})\gt 0\)

Fundamental Trigonometric Identities

Trigonometric identities are equations involving sine, cosine, tangent, etc, that hold true for any angle for which they are defined.

The most fundamental trigonometric identities are the following:

\(\sin^2(ω)+\cos^2(ω)=1\)

This is the most fundamental trigonometric identity and follows directly from the Pythagorean theorem.

Specifically, choosing an arbitrary point \((x,y)\) on the unit circle forms a right triangle with legs of length \(|x|\) and \(|y|.\)

Using the Pythagorean theorem, we find that the hypotenuse has length \(\sqrt{|x|^2+|y|^2}=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\)

However, the hypotenuse is also a radius of the unit circle, so it is equal to \(1.\)

Therefore, \(\sqrt{x^2+y^2}=1\iff x^2+y^2=1.\)

And since \(x=\cos(ω)\) and \(y=\sin(ω),\) it follows that \(\sin^2(ω)+\cos^2(ω)=1\)

Note

The notation \(\sin^2(ω)\) and \(\cos^2(ω)\) means \((\sin(ω))^2\) and \((\cos(ω))^2\) respectively.

We use the former notation simply to avoid writing extra parentheses.

\(\sin^2(ω)=1-\cos^2(ω)\)

This identity is obtained by solving the first one for \(\sin^2(ω).\)

\(\cos^2(ω)=1-\sin^2(ω)\)

This identity is obtained by solving the first one for \(\cos^2(ω).\)

\(\tan(ω)=\cfrac{\sin(ω)}{\cos(ω)}\)

This identity follows from the definition of tangent on the unit circle as \(\tan(ω)=\cfrac{y}{x}\) with \(x\neq 0.\)

Specifically, since \(y=\sin(ω)\) and \(x=\cos(ω),\) we have \(\tan(ω)=\cfrac{y}{x}=\cfrac{\sin(ω)}{\cos(ω)}\)

\(\cot(ω)=\cfrac{\cos(ω)}{\sin(ω)}\)

This identity follows from the definition of cotangent on the unit circle as \(\cot(ω)=\cfrac{x}{y}\) with \(y\neq 0.\)

Specifically, since \(y=\sin(ω)\) and \(x=\cos(ω),\) we have \(\cot(ω)=\cfrac{x}{y}=\cfrac{\cos(ω)}{\sin(ω)}\)

\(\tan(ω)\cdot \cot(ω)=1\)

This identity follows from the previous two, since:

\(\quad ! \quad \tan(ω)\cdot \cot(ω)=\cfrac{\sin(ω)}{\cos(ω)}\cdot\cfrac{\cos(ω)}{\sin(ω)}=1\)

\(\tan(ω)=\cfrac{1}{\cot(ω)}\)

This identity is obtained by solving \(\tan(ω)\cdot \cot(ω)=1\) for \(\tan(ω).\)

\(\cot(ω)=\cfrac{1}{\tan(ω)}\)

This identity is obtained by solving \(\tan(ω)\cdot \cot(ω)=1\) for \(\cot(ω).\)

\(\csc(ω)=\cfrac{1}{\sin(ω)}\)

This identity follows from the definition of cosecant on the unit circle as \(\csc(ω)=\cfrac{1}{y}\) with \(y\neq 0.\)

Specifically, since \(y=\sin(ω),\) we have \(\csc(ω)=\cfrac{1}{y}=\cfrac{1}{\sin(ω)}\)

\(\sec(ω)=\cfrac{1}{\cos(ω)}\)

This identity follows from the definition of secant on the unit circle as \(\sec(ω)=\cfrac{1}{x}\) with \(x\neq 0.\)

Specifically, since \(x=\cos(ω),\) we have \(\sec(ω)=\cfrac{1}{x}=\cfrac{1}{\cos(ω)}\)

\(\tan^2(ω)+1=\sec^2(ω)\)

By dividing everything in the identity \(\sin^2(ω)+\cos^2(ω)=1\) by \(\cos^2(ω),\) we obtain:

\(\quad ! \quad \cfrac{\sin^2(ω)}{\cos^2(ω)}+\cfrac{\cos^2(ω)}{\cos^2(ω)}=\cfrac{1}{\cos^2(ω)}\iff \tan^2(ω)+1=\sec^2(ω)\)

\(\cot^2(ω)+1=\csc^2(ω)\)

By dividing everything in the identity \(\sin^2(ω)+\cos^2(ω)=1\) by \(\sin^2(ω),\) we obtain:

\(\quad ! \quad \cfrac{\sin^2(ω)}{\sin^2(ω)}+\cfrac{\cos^2(ω)}{\sin^2(ω)}=\cfrac{1}{\sin^2(ω)}\iff \cot^2(ω)+1=\csc^2(ω)\)

Common Exercise

A very common exercise in trigonometry is the following:

  • We are given an angle \(ω,\) knowing only which quadrant it lies in.
  • We are given one of its trigonometric values.
  • We are asked to find the remaining trigonometric values of the angle \(ω.\)

There are two cases:

Given \(\sin(ω)\) or \(\cos(ω)\)

For example, suppose \(\sin(ω)=\cfrac{3}{5}\) for an angle \(0\lt ω\lt \cfrac{π}{2}\) (first quadrant), and we want to find \(\cos(ω),\) \(\tan(ω),\) \(\csc(ω),\) \(\sec(ω),\) and \(\cot(ω).\)

First, we use the identity \(\sin^2(ω)+\cos^2(ω)=1\) and substitute the known trigonometric value into it.

Next, we solve for the unknown trigonometric value.

That is:

\(\quad ! \quad \sin^2(ω)+\cos^2(ω)=1\iff \left(\cfrac{3}{5}\right)^2+\cos^2(ω)=1\iff\cfrac{9}{25}+\cos^2(ω)=1\iff \cos^2(ω)=1-\cfrac{9}{25}\iff \cos^2(ω)=\cfrac{16}{25}\)

Recall that \(x^2=a\iff x=-\sqrt{a}\,\,\) or \(\,\,x=\sqrt{a},\) for \(a\ge 0.\)

Thus, we get \(\cos(ω)=-\cfrac{4}{5}\,\,\) or \(\,\,\cos(ω)=\cfrac{4}{5}\)

To choose the correct sign, we look at the quadrant in which the angle \(ω\) lies.

In this example, we need the sign of cosine in the first quadrant.

In the first quadrant, all trigonometric values are positive, so \(\cos(ω)=\cfrac{4}{5}\)

Next, we calculate the tangent using \(\tan(ω)=\cfrac{\sin(ω)}{\cos(ω)}\)

In this example, \(\tan(ω)=\cfrac{\frac{3}{5}}{\frac{4}{5}}=\cfrac{3}{4}\)

Finally, we find \(\csc(ω),\) \(\sec(ω),\) and \(\cot(ω)\) easily, since they are the reciprocals of the other three.

In this example:

  • \(\csc(ω)=\cfrac{5}{3}\)
  • \(\sec(ω)=\cfrac{5}{4}\)
  • \(\cot(ω)=\cfrac{4}{3}\)

The exact same process is followed when \(\cos(ω)\) is given initially.

Note

If \(\csc(ω)\) or \(\sec(ω)\) were given initially, then we would automatically know \(\sin(ω)\) or \(\cos(ω)\) respectively, so the process would be the same.

Given \(\tan(ω)\)

For example, suppose \(\tan(ω)=13\) for an angle \(π\lt ω\lt \cfrac{3π}{2}\) (third quadrant), and we want to find \(\sin(ω),\) \(\cos(ω),\) \(\csc(ω),\) \(\sec(ω),\) and \(\cot(ω).\)

First, we use the definition \(\tan(ω)=\cfrac{\sin(ω)}{\cos(ω)}\) and substitute the given value of \(\tan(ω).\)

Then, we either solve for sine or cosine.

That is:

\(\quad ! \quad \tan(ω)=\cfrac{\sin(ω)}{\cos(ω)}\iff 13=\cfrac{\sin(ω)}{\cos(ω)}\iff \sin(ω)=13\cos(ω)\)

Next, we use the identity \(\sin^2(ω)+\cos^2(ω)=1\) and substitute the relation we just found.

That is:

\(\quad ! \quad \sin^2(ω)+\cos^2(ω)=1\iff (13\cos(ω))^2+\cos^2(ω)=1\iff 169\cos^2(ω)+\cos^2(ω)=1\iff 170\cos^2(ω)=1\iff \cos^2(ω)=\cfrac{1}{170}\)

Recall that \(x^2=a\iff x=-\sqrt{a}\,\,\) or \(\,\,x=\sqrt{a},\) for \(a\ge 0.\)

Thus, we get \(\cos(ω)=-\cfrac{1}{\sqrt{170}}\,\,\) or \(\,\,\cos(ω)=\cfrac{1}{\sqrt{170}}\)

To choose the correct sign, we look at the quadrant in which the angle \(ω\) lies.

In this example, we need the sign of cosine in the third quadrant.

In the third quadrant, only tangent and cotangent are positive, so cosine is negative.

Therefore, \(\cos(ω)=-\cfrac{1}{\sqrt{170}}\)

Then, we substitute the trigonometric value we found back into the relation we obtained at the beginning.

In this example, \(\sin(ω)=13\cos(ω)\iff\) \(\sin(ω)=-\cfrac{13}{\sqrt{170}}\)

Finally, we find \(\csc(ω),\) \(\sec(ω),\) and \(\cot(ω)\) easily, since they are the reciprocals of the other three.

In this example:

  • \(\csc(ω)=-\cfrac{\sqrt{170}}{13}\)
  • \(\sec(ω)=-\sqrt{170}\)
  • \(\cot(ω)=\cfrac{1}{13}\)

The exact same process is followed when \(\cot(ω)\) is given initially.

Reduction To The First Quadrant

Reduction to the first quadrant means expressing the trigonometric values of angles outside the first quadrant in terms of the corresponding values of a first-quadrant reference angle.

Opposite Angles

On the unit circle, imagine rotating a line segment through an angle \(ω\) to reach a point \((x,y).\)

Because the unit circle is symmetric with respect to the \(x\)-axis, if we rotate the line segment through the opposite angle, \(-ω,\) we reach the point \((x,-y),\) which is the reflection of \((x,y)\) across the \(x\)-axis.

For example:

Since the two points have the same \(x\)-coordinate, the cosines of the angles are also equal. That is, \(\cos(-ω)=\cos(ω).\)

Conversely, since the two points have opposite \(y\)-coordinates, the sines of the angles are also opposites. That is, \(\sin(-ω)=-\sin(ω).\)

As for the others:

\(\quad ! \quad \tan(-ω)=\cfrac{\sin(-ω)}{\cos(-ω)}=\cfrac{-\sin(ω)}{\cos(ω)}=-\cfrac{\sin(ω)}{\cos(ω)}=-\tan(ω)\)

\(\quad ! \quad \csc(-ω)=\cfrac{1}{\sin(-ω)}=\cfrac{1}{-\sin(ω)}=-\cfrac{1}{\sin(ω)}=-\csc(ω)\)

\(\quad ! \quad \sec(-ω)=\cfrac{1}{\cos(-ω)}=\cfrac{1}{\cos(ω)}=\sec(ω)\)

\(\quad ! \quad \cot(-ω)=\cfrac{\cos(-ω)}{\sin(-ω)}=\cfrac{\cos(ω)}{-\sin(ω)}=-\cfrac{\cos(ω)}{\sin(ω)}=-\cot(ω)\)

Conclusion

For any angle \(ω,\) the following hold true:

  • \(\sin(-ω)=-\sin(ω)\)
  • \(\cos(-ω)=\cos(ω)\)
  • \(\tan(-ω)=-\tan(ω)\)
  • \(\csc(-ω)=-\csc(ω)\)
  • \(\sec(-ω)=\sec(ω)\)
  • \(\cot(-ω)=-\cot(ω)\)

Thus, instead of converting the angle \(-ω\) into \(2π-ω\) as shown earlier, we can simply pull the minus sign out of the trigonometric functions, except for cosine, where the minus sign is dropped entirely.

Examples
  • \(\sin(-\frac{π}{2})=-\sin(\frac{π}{2})=-1\)
  • \(\cos(-π)=\cos(π)=-1\)
  • \(\tan(-\frac{π}{3})=-\tan(\frac{π}{3})=-\sqrt{3}\)

Supplementary Angles

On the unit circle, imagine rotating a line segment through an angle \(ω\) to reach a point \((x,y).\)

Because the unit circle is symmetric with respect to the \(y\)-axis, if we rotate the line segment through its supplementary angle, \(π-ω,\) we reach the point \((-x,y),\) which is the reflection of \((x,y)\) across the \(y\)-axis.

For example:

Since the two points have the same \(y\)-coordinate, the sines of the angles are also equal. That is, \(\sin(π-ω)=\sin(ω).\)

Conversely, since the two points have opposite \(x\)-coordinates, the cosines of the angles are also opposites. That is, \(\cos(π-ω)=-\cos(ω).\)

As for the others:

\(\quad ! \quad \tan(π-ω)=\cfrac{\sin(π-ω)}{\cos(π-ω)}=\cfrac{\sin(ω)}{-\cos(ω)}=-\cfrac{\sin(ω)}{\cos(ω)}=-\tan(ω)\)

\(\quad ! \quad \csc(π-ω)=\cfrac{1}{\sin(π-ω)}=\cfrac{1}{\sin(ω)}=\csc(ω)\)

\(\quad ! \quad \sec(π-ω)=\cfrac{1}{\cos(π-ω)}=\cfrac{1}{-\cos(ω)}=-\cfrac{1}{\cos(ω)}=-\sec(ω)\)

\(\quad ! \quad \cot(π-ω)=\cfrac{\cos(π-ω)}{\sin(π-ω)}=\cfrac{-\cos(ω)}{\sin(ω)}=-\cfrac{\cos(ω)}{\sin(ω)}=-\cot(ω)\)

Conclusion

For any angle \(ω,\) the following hold true:

  • \(\sin(π-ω)=\sin(ω)\)
  • \(\cos(π-ω)=-\cos(ω)\)
  • \(\tan(π-ω)=-\tan(ω)\)
  • \(\csc(π-ω)=\csc(ω)\)
  • \(\sec(π-ω)=-\sec(ω)\)
  • \(\cot(π-ω)=-\cot(ω)\)

These properties hold for any angle, but they are particularly useful when \(ω\) lies in the first quadrant, allowing us to reduce an angle \(π-ω\) (in the second quadrant) to a first-quadrant angle.

Examples
  • \(\sin(\frac{3π}{4})=\sin(π-\frac{π}{4})=\) \(\sin(\frac{π}{4})=\cfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\)
  • \(\cos(\frac{2π}{3})=\cos(π-\frac{π}{3})=\) \(-\cos(\frac{π}{3})=-\cfrac{1}{2}\)
  • \(\tan(\frac{5π}{6})=\tan(π-\frac{π}{6})=\) \(-\tan(\frac{π}{6})=-\cfrac{\sqrt{3}}{3}\)

Important Note

If we have \(π+ω\) instead of \(π-ω,\) we can simply rewrite it as \(π-(-ω)\) and apply the properties above.

For example:

\(\quad ! \quad \sin(\frac{5π}{4})=\sin(π+\frac{π}{4})=\sin(π-(-\frac{π}{4}))=\sin(-\frac{π}{4})=-\sin(\frac{π}{4})=-\cfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\)

Complementary Angles

On the unit circle, imagine rotating a line segment through an angle \(ω\) to reach a point \((x,y).\)

Because the unit circle is symmetric with respect to the line \(y=x,\) if we rotate the line segment through the angle \(\cfrac{π}{2}-ω,\) we reach the point \((y,x),\) which is the reflection of \((x,y)\) across the line \(y=x.\)

Since the \(x\)-coordinate of one point equals the \(y\)-coordinate of the other, the cosine of one angle equals the sine of the other.

That is, \(\sin(\frac{π}{2}-ω)=\cos(ω)\) and \(\cos(\frac{π}{2}-ω)=\sin(ω)\)

As for the others:

\(\quad ! \quad \tan(\frac{π}{2}-ω)=\cfrac{\sin(\frac{π}{2}-ω)}{\cos(\frac{π}{2}-ω)}=\cfrac{\cos(ω)}{\sin(ω)}=\cot(ω)\)

\(\quad ! \quad \csc(\frac{π}{2}-ω)=\cfrac{1}{\sin(\frac{π}{2}-ω)}=\cfrac{1}{\cos(ω)}=\sec(ω)\)

\(\quad ! \quad \sec(\frac{π}{2}-ω)=\cfrac{1}{\cos(\frac{π}{2}-ω)}=\cfrac{1}{\sin(ω)}=\csc(ω)\)

\(\quad ! \quad \cot(\frac{π}{2}-ω)=\cfrac{\cos(\frac{π}{2}-ω)}{\sin(\frac{π}{2}-ω)}=\cfrac{\sin(ω)}{\cos(ω)}=\tan(ω)\)

Conclusion

For any angle \(ω,\) the following hold true:

  • \(\sin(\cfrac{π}{2}-ω)=\cos(ω)\)
  • \(\cos(\cfrac{π}{2}-ω)=\sin(ω)\)
  • \(\tan(\cfrac{π}{2}-ω)=\cot(ω)\)
  • \(\cot(\cfrac{π}{2}-ω)=\tan(ω)\)
  • \(\csc(\cfrac{π}{2}-ω)=\sec(ω)\)
  • \(\sec(\cfrac{π}{2}-ω)=\csc(ω)\)
Examples
  • \(\sin(\frac{π}{3})=\sin(\frac{π}{2}-\frac{π}{6})=\) \(\cos(\frac{π}{6})=\cfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\)
  • \(\tan(\frac{π}{6})=\tan(\frac{π}{2}-\frac{π}{3})=\) \(\cot(\frac{π}{3})=\cfrac{\sqrt{3}}{3}\)

Important Note

If we have \(\cfrac{π}{2}+ω\) instead of \(\cfrac{π}{2}-ω,\) we can simply rewrite it as \(\cfrac{π}{2}-(-ω)\) and apply the properties above.

Shortcut Rule For Reductions

Suppose we want to evaluate a trigonometric function of an angle in the form \(π\pm ω,\) \(\cfrac{π}{2}\pm ω,\) or \(\cfrac{3π}{2}\pm ω.\)

  • If the angle involves \(π,\) the angle reduces to \(ω\) and the function remains unchanged (i.e., sine stays sine, cosine stays cosine, etc.).
  • If the angle involves \(\cfrac{π}{2}\) or \(\cfrac{3π}{2},\) the angle reduces to \(ω\) and the function changes co-function status:
    • Sine becomes cosine
    • Cosine becomes sine
    • Tangent becomes cotangent
    • Cotangent becomes tangent
    • Secant becomes cosecant
    • Cosecant becomes secant
  • The sign \((+\) or \(−)\) of the result depends on the quadrant where the original angle terminates and the original function (before changing, if it changed):
    • In the quadrant I, all functions are positive.
    • In the quadrant II, only sine and cosecant are positive.
    • In the quadrant III, only tangent and cotangent are positive.
    • In the quadrant IV, only cosine and secant are positive.
  • Therefore, if the original function is positive in that quadrant, the result is positive. If it is negative, the result is negative (i.e., attach a minus sign).
Examples

\(1)\) For \(\sin(\frac{3π}{2}-\frac{π}{3}),\) we reason as follows:

  • The expression involves \(\cfrac{3π}{2},\) so sine changes to cosine and the angle becomes \(\cfrac{π}{3}.\)
  • Regarding the sign, the angle \(\cfrac{3π}{2}-\cfrac{π}{3}\) lies in the third quadrant, where sine is negative, so we attach a minus sign to the result.

That is, \(\sin(\frac{3π}{2}-\frac{π}{3})=-\cos(\frac{π}{3})=-\cfrac{1}{2}\)

\(2)\) For \(\cos(π+\frac{π}{4}),\) we reason as follows:

  • The expression involves \(π,\) so cosine does not change and the angle becomes \(\cfrac{π}{4}.\)
  • Regarding the sign, the angle \(π+\cfrac{π}{4}\) lies in the third quadrant, where cosine is negative, so we attach a minus sign to the result.

That is, \(\cos(π+\frac{π}{4})=-\cos(\frac{π}{4})=-\cfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\)

\(3)\) For \(\tan(\frac{π}{2}-\frac{π}{4}),\) we reason as follows:

  • The expression involves \(\cfrac{π}{2},\) so tangent changes to cotangent and the angle becomes \(\cfrac{π}{4}.\)
  • Regarding the sign, the angle \(\cfrac{π}{2}-\cfrac{π}{4}\) lies in the first quadrant, where tangent is positive.

That is, \(\tan(\frac{π}{2}-\frac{π}{4})=\cot(\frac{π}{4})=1\)

Periodic Functions

A function \(f\) with domain \(D_f\) is called periodic if there exists \(T\in\mathbb{R}\) such that for every \(x\in D_f:\)

  • \(x-T\in D_f\)
  • \(x+T\in D_f\)
  • \(f(x+T)=f(x)\)

The fact that a function is periodic means that its values repeat every \(T\) units.

Also, \(T\) is called the period of the function.

You will better understand what we mean when you see the graphs of the trigonometric functions.

The Function \(\sin(x)\)

The function \(\sin(x)\) has the entire \(\mathbb{R}\) as its domain, as it accepts any number \(x\) rad as input and returns the sine of that number.

Its graph is as follows:

Looking at its graph, we see everything we expected to see for the sine function based on the unit circle.

Specifically, \(\sin(x):\)

  • Is periodic with period \(T=2π,\) since as soon as one full turn (\(2π\) rad) is completed, the values of sine repeat. That is, the section of the graph of \(\sin(x)\) in the interval \([0,2π]\) is identical to those in the intervals \([-2π,0],\) \([2π,4π],\) and generally in every interval \([k\cdot 2π,(k+1)\cdot 2π]\) with \(k\in\mathbb{Z}.\)
  • Its graph is bounded between the horizontal lines \(y=1\) and \(y=-1,\) since \(-1\le \sin(x)\le 1\) holds for every \(x\in\mathbb{R}.\)
  • Is positive in the interval \((0,π),\) and generally in every interval \((k\cdot 2π,π+k\cdot 2π)\) with \(k\in\mathbb{Z},\) as these correspond to angles ending in the first and second quadrants, where the sine is positive.
  • Is negative in the interval \((π,2π),\) and generally in every interval \(\left(π+k\cdot 2π,2π+k\cdot 2π\right)\) with \(k\in\mathbb{Z},\) as these correspond to angles ending in the third and fourth quadrants, where the sine is negative.
  • Equals zero at the points \(k\cdot π\) for every \(k\in\mathbb{Z}.\) That is, at the angles corresponding to the points \((1,0)\) and \((-1,0)\) on the unit circle.
  • Is strictly increasing in the interval \(\left[-\cfrac{π}{2},\cfrac{π}{2}\right],\) and generally in every interval \(\left[-\cfrac{π}{2}+k\cdot 2π,\cfrac{π}{2}+k\cdot 2π\right]\) with \(k\in\mathbb{Z},\) as these correspond to angles ending in the first and fourth quadrants, where the values of sine increase as the angle \(x\) increases.
  • Is strictly decreasing in the interval \(\left[\cfrac{π}{2},\cfrac{3π}{2}\right],\) and generally in every interval \(\left[\cfrac{π}{2}+k\cdot 2π,\cfrac{3π}{2}+k\cdot 2π\right]\) with \(k\in\mathbb{Z},\) as these correspond to angles ending in the second and third quadrants, where the values of sine decrease as the angle \(x\) increases.
  • Has a global maximum equal to \(1\) at the points \(\cfrac{π}{2}+k\cdot 2π\) for every \(k\in\mathbb{Z}.\) That is, at the angles corresponding to the point \((0,1)\) on the unit circle.
  • Has a global minimum equal to \(-1\) at the points \(\cfrac{3π}{2}+k\cdot 2π\) for every \(k\in\mathbb{Z}.\) That is, at the angles corresponding to the point \((0,-1)\) on the unit circle.
  • Is odd, since \(D_f=\mathbb{R}\) and \(\sin(-x)=-\sin(x)\) for every \(x\in\mathbb{R}.\)

Note

Since the function is periodic with period \(T=2π,\) if we are asked to plot its graph, it is sufficient to sketch it in the interval \([0,2π].\)

Then, the same pattern repeats to the right and to the left.

The Function \(\cos(x)\)

The function \(\cos(x)\) has the entire \(\mathbb{R}\) as its domain, as it accepts any number \(x\) rad as input and returns the cosine of that number.

Its graph is as follows:

Looking at its graph, we see everything we expected to see for the cosine function based on the unit circle.

Specifically, \(\cos(x):\)

  • Is periodic with period \(T=2π,\) since as soon as one full turn (\(2π\) rad) is completed, the values of cosine repeat. That is, the section of the graph of \(\cos(x)\) in the interval \([0,2π]\) is identical to those in the intervals \([-2π,0],\) \([2π,4π],\) and generally in every interval \([k\cdot 2π,(k+1)\cdot 2π]\) with \(k\in\mathbb{Z}.\)
  • Its graph is bounded between the horizontal lines \(y=1\) and \(y=-1,\) since \(-1\le \cos(x)\le 1\) holds for every \(x\in\mathbb{R}.\)
  • Is positive in the interval \(\left(-\cfrac{π}{2},\cfrac{π}{2}\right),\) and generally in every interval \(\left(-\cfrac{π}{2}+k\cdot 2π,\cfrac{π}{2}+k\cdot 2π\right)\) with \(k\in\mathbb{Z},\) as these correspond to angles ending in the first and fourth quadrants, where the cosine is positive.
  • Is negative in the interval \(\left(\cfrac{π}{2},\cfrac{3π}{2}\right),\) and generally in every interval \(\left(\cfrac{π}{2}+k\cdot 2π,\cfrac{3π}{2}+k\cdot 2π\right)\) with \(k\in\mathbb{Z},\) as these correspond to angles ending in the second and third quadrants, where the cosine is negative.
  • Equals zero at the points \(k\cdot π+\cfrac{π}{2}\) for every \(k\in\mathbb{Z}.\) That is, at the angles corresponding to the points \((0,1)\) and \((0,-1)\) on the unit circle.
  • Is strictly increasing in the interval \([π,2π],\) and generally in every interval \([π+k\cdot 2π,2π+k\cdot 2π]\) with \(k\in\mathbb{Z},\) as these correspond to angles ending in the third and fourth quadrants, where the values of cosine increase as the angle \(x\) increases.
  • Is strictly decreasing in the interval \([0,π],\) and generally in every interval \([k\cdot 2π,π+k\cdot 2π]\) with \(k\in\mathbb{Z},\) as these correspond to angles ending in the first and second quadrants, where the values of cosine decrease as the angle \(x\) increases.
  • Has a global maximum equal to \(1\) at the points \(k\cdot 2π\) for every \(k\in\mathbb{Z}.\) That is, at the angles corresponding to the point \((1,0)\) on the unit circle.
  • Has a global minimum equal to \(-1\) at the points \(k\cdot 2π+π\) for every \(k\in\mathbb{Z}.\) That is, at the angles corresponding to the point \((-1,0)\) on the unit circle.
  • Is even, since \(D_f=\mathbb{R}\) and \(\cos(-x)=\cos(x)\) for every \(x\in\mathbb{R}.\)

Note \(1\)

Since the function is periodic with period \(T=2π,\) if we are asked to plot its graph, it is sufficient to sketch it in the interval \([0,2π].\)

Then, the same pattern repeats to the right and to the left.

Note \(2\)

If you noticed, the graph of \(\cos(x)\) has the same shape as the graph of \(\sin(x),\) it is simply shifted \(\cfrac{π}{2}\) units to the left.

This was expected, because as we saw in the reduction to the first quadrant, \(\sin(x+\frac{π}{2})=\cos(x).\)

The Function \(\tan(x)\)

Because the "main characters" of trigonometry are the functions \(\sin(x)\) and \(\cos(x),\) we will cover the tangent function briefly.

Specifically:

Tangent is defined as \(\tan(x)=\cfrac{\sin(x)}{\cos(x)}\)

Its domain is \(\{x\in\mathbb{R}\mid \cos(x)\neq 0\},\) that is, \(\mathbb{R}\setminus\left\{\cfrac{π}{2}+k\cdot π\,,\,k\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}.\)

Its graph is as follows:

As we can see, \(\tan(x):\)

  • Is periodic with period \(T=π.\)
  • Is strictly increasing in the interval \(\left(-\cfrac{π}{2},\cfrac{π}{2}\right),\) and generally in every interval \(\left(-\cfrac{π}{2}+k\cdot π,\cfrac{π}{2}+k\cdot π\right).\)
  • Has no extrema.
  • Equals zero at the points \(k\cdot π,\) since \(\sin(x)\) equals zero there.
  • Has vertical asymptotes at all lines \(x=\cfrac{π}{2}+k\cdot π\) for every \(k\in\mathbb{Z}.\)
  • Is odd, since its domain is symmetric and \(\tan(-x)=-\tan(x)\) for every \(x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\left\{k\cdot π+\cfrac{π}{2}\right\}.\)

The Function \(\csc(x)\)

Since \(\csc(x)\) is just the reciprocal of \(\sin(x),\) we will cover it briefly.

Specifically:

Cosecant is defined as \(\csc(x)=\cfrac{1}{\sin(x)}\)

Its domain is \(\{x\in\mathbb{R}\mid \sin(x)\neq 0\},\) that is, \(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{k\cdot π\,,\,k\in\mathbb{Z}\}.\)

Its graph is as follows:

As we can see, \(\csc(x):\)

  • Is periodic with period \(T=2π.\)
  • Is strictly decreasing in the intervals \(\left(0,\cfrac{π}{2}\right)\) and \(\left(\cfrac{3π}{2},2π\right),\) and generally in every interval \(\left(k\cdot 2π,\cfrac{π}{2}+k\cdot 2π\right)\) and \(\left(\cfrac{3π}{2}+k\cdot 2π,2π+k\cdot 2π\right),\) where \(k\in\mathbb{Z}.\)
  • Is strictly increasing in the intervals \(\left(\cfrac{π}{2},π\right)\) and \(\left(π,\cfrac{3π}{2}\right),\) and generally in every interval \(\left(\cfrac{π}{2}+k\cdot 2π,π+k\cdot 2π\right)\) and \(\left(π+k\cdot 2π,\cfrac{3π}{2}+k\cdot 2π\right),\) where \(k\in\mathbb{Z}.\)
  • Has local minima equal to \(1\) at the points \(x=\cfrac{π}{2}+k\cdot 2π,\) and local maxima equal to \(-1\) at the points \(x=\cfrac{3π}{2}+k\cdot 2π,\) where \(k\in\mathbb{Z}.\)
  • Never equals zero.
  • Has vertical asymptotes at all lines \(x=k\cdot π\) for every \(k\in\mathbb{Z}.\)
  • Is odd, since its domain is symmetric and \(\csc(-x)=-\csc(x)\) for every \(x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{k\cdot π\}.\)

The Function \(\sec(x)\)

Specifically:

Secant is defined as \(\sec(x)=\cfrac{1}{\cos(x)}\)

Its domain is \(\{x\in\mathbb{R}\mid \cos(x)\neq 0\},\) that is, \(\mathbb{R}\setminus\left\{\cfrac{π}{2}+k\cdot π\,,\,k\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}.\)

Its graph is as follows:

As we can see, \(\sec(x):\)

  • Is periodic with period \(T=2π.\)
  • Is strictly increasing in the intervals \(\left(0,\cfrac{π}{2}\right)\) and \(\left(\cfrac{π}{2},π\right),\) and generally in every interval \(\left(k\cdot 2π,\cfrac{π}{2}+k\cdot 2π\right)\) and \(\left(\cfrac{π}{2}+k\cdot 2π,π+k\cdot 2π\right),\) where \(k\in\mathbb{Z}.\)
  • Is strictly decreasing in the intervals \(\left(π,\cfrac{3π}{2}\right)\) and \(\left(\cfrac{3π}{2},2π\right),\) and generally in every interval \(\left(π+k\cdot 2π,\cfrac{3π}{2}+k\cdot 2π\right)\) and \(\left(\cfrac{3π}{2}+k\cdot 2π,2π+k\cdot 2π\right),\) where \(k\in\mathbb{Z}.\)
  • Has local minima equal to \(1\) at the points \(x=k\cdot 2π,\) and local maxima equal to \(-1\) at the points \(x=π+k\cdot 2π,\) where \(k\in\mathbb{Z}.\)
  • Never equals zero.

  • Is even, since its domain is symmetric and \(\sec(-x)=\sec(x)\) for every \(x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\left\{\cfrac{π}{2}+k\cdot π\right\}.\)

The Function \(\cot(x)\)

Since \(\cot(x)\) is just the reciprocal of \(\tan(x),\) we will cover it briefly.

Specifically:

Cotangent is defined as \(\cot(x)=\cfrac{\cos(x)}{\sin(x)}\)

Its domain is \(\{x\in\mathbb{R}\mid \sin(x)\neq 0\},\) that is, \(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{k\cdot π\,,\,k\in\mathbb{Z}\}.\)

Its graph is as follows:

As we can see, \(\cot(x):\)

  • Is periodic with period \(T=π.\)
  • Is strictly decreasing in the interval \((0,π),\) and generally in every interval \((k\cdot π,π+k\cdot π).\)
  • Has no extrema.
  • Equals zero at the points \(k\cdot π+\cfrac{π}{2},\) since \(\cos(x)\) equals zero there.
  • Has vertical asymptotes at all lines \(x=k\cdot π\) for every \(k\in\mathbb{Z}.\)
  • Finally, it is odd, since its domain is symmetric and \(\cot(-x)=-\cot(x)\) for every \(x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{k\cdot π\}.\)

The Functions \(a+b\cdot \sin(ωx)\) And \(a+b\cdot \cos(ωx)\) With \(ω\gt 0\)

These functions have three key differences compared to \(\sin(x)\) and \(\cos(x)\) respectively:

  • Their period is \(T=\cfrac{2π}{ω}.\) This means that their pattern completes faster, if \(ω\gt 1,\) or slower, if \(0\lt ω\lt 1.\) Specifically, instead of taking the interval \([0,2π],\) it takes the interval \(\left[0,\cfrac{2π}{ω}\right].\)
  • Their graphs are not bounded between the lines \(y=1\) and \(y=-1,\) but between \(y=a+|b|\) and \(y=a-|b|.\)
  • If \(b\lt 0,\) then the intervals of monotonicity of the functions \(a+b\cdot \sin(ωx)\) and \(a+b\cdot \cos(ωx)\) are inverted relative to those of \(\sin(x)\) and \(\cos(x)\) respectively.

How To Graph The Functions \(a+b\cdot \sin(ωx)\) And \(a+b\cdot \cos(ωx)\)

  • The functions are periodic, so we draw their graphs over an interval of one period, usually \(\left[0,\cfrac{2π}{ω}\right].\)
  • We divide this interval into four parts: \(\left[0,\cfrac{π}{2ω}\right],\) \(\left[\cfrac{π}{2ω},\cfrac{π}{ω}\right],\) \(\left[\cfrac{π}{ω},\cfrac{3π}{2ω}\right]\) and \(\left[\cfrac{3π}{2ω},\cfrac{2π}{ω}\right].\)
    These are the four subintervals into which we would also divide the functions \(\sin(x)\) and \(\cos(x),\) namely \(\left[0,\cfrac{π}{2}\right],\) \(\left[\cfrac{π}{2},π\right],\) \(\left[π,\cfrac{3π}{2}\right]\) and \(\left[\cfrac{3π}{2},2π\right],\) simply divided by \(ω.\)
  • If \(b\gt 0,\) then the monotonicity of the functions in each interval will be the same as the monotonicity of the corresponding base function in that interval. If \(b\lt 0,\) then the monotonicity is inverted (e.g., if the base function is strictly increasing in the first of the four intervals, then the function we are plotting will be strictly decreasing in that first interval).
  • Finally, based on the above, we draw a shape similar to the base functions, but bounded between the lines \(y=a+|b|\) and \(y=a-|b|,\) with the line \(y=a\) bisecting the shape (just as the \(x\)-axis does for the base functions).

Example

We are given a function \(f(x)=a+b\cdot \sin(ωx)\) for which we know that:

  • \(b\lt 0\)
  • It has a period of \(π.\)
  • Its graph passes through the point \((π,3).\)
  • It has a global minimum equal to \(2.\)

Find:

  • Find \(a,b,ω.\)
  • Plot the graph of the function for one period.
Solution
  • We know that the period of the function is \(π\) and \(T=\cfrac{2π}{ω},\) so \(π=\cfrac{2π}{ω}\iff ω=2.\)
  • The graph of the function passes through the point \((π,3)\) and we found \(ω=2,\) so \(a+b\cdot \sin(2π)=3\iff\) \(a=3-0\iff a=3.\)
  • The function has a global minimum of \(2,\) so \(a-|b|=2.\) Since \(b\lt 0\) and \(a=3,\) we get \(3+b=2\iff b=-1.\)

Therefore, the function is \(f(x)=3-\sin(2x).\)

Its graph will have a period of \(π,\) so we will plot it in the interval \([0,π].\)

The four subintervals of this interval are:

  • \(\left[0,\cfrac{π}{4}\right]\)
  • \(\left[\cfrac{π}{4},\cfrac{π}{2}\right]\)
  • \(\left[\cfrac{π}{2},\cfrac{3π}{4}\right]\)
  • \(\left[\cfrac{3π}{4},π\right]\)

Since \(b\lt 0,\) the monotonicity will be inverted relative to \(\sin(x).\) That is, \(f(x)=3-\sin(2x)\) will be:

  • Strictly decreasing in \(\left[0,\cfrac{π}{4}\right]\)
  • Strictly increasing in \(\left[\cfrac{π}{4},\cfrac{π}{2}\right]\)
  • Strictly increasing in \(\left[\cfrac{π}{2},\cfrac{3π}{4}\right]\)
  • Strictly decreasing in \(\left[\cfrac{3π}{4},π\right]\)

Finally, the graph of \(f\) will lie between the lines \(y=2\) and \(y=4,\) with \(y=3\) bisecting it.

Therefore:

We would follow the exact same process if we had a function of the form \(a+b\cdot \cos(ωx),\) making the appropriate adjustments for cosine.

Trigonometric Equations

The most basic forms of trigonometric equations are the following:

  • \(\sin(x)=\sin(θ)\)
  • \(\cos(x)=\cos(θ)\)
  • \(\tan(x)=\tan(θ)\)

With \(θ\in\mathbb{R}.\)

All of them are solved in the same way: we consider what relationship two angles must have in order to have the same trigonometric function value, and we add \(k\cdot 2π\) to the solution for the first two, or \(k\cdot π\) for the third, since angles differing by one or more periods are equivalent.

The Equation \(\sin(x)=\sin(θ)\)

Recall that for two angles to have the same sine, they must either be equal or supplementary.

Thus, the equation has solutions \(x=θ\) or \(x=π-θ.\)

However, since adding \(k\cdot 2π\) to an angle leaves its sine unchanged, we ultimately get:

\(\quad ! \quad \sin(x)=\sin(θ)\iff\begin{cases}x=θ+k\cdot 2π\\\text{ or}\\x=π-θ+k\cdot 2π\end{cases}\) with \(k\in\mathbb{Z}.\)

The Equation \(\csc(x)=\csc(θ)\)

The equation \(\csc(x)=\csc(θ)\) can be rewritten as \(\cfrac{1}{\sin(x)}=\cfrac{1}{\sin(θ)}\iff\) \(\sin(x)=\sin(θ)\)

Therefore, it has the same solutions as \(\sin(x)=\sin(θ):\)

\(\quad ! \quad \csc(x)=\csc(θ)\iff\begin{cases}x=θ+k\cdot 2π\\\text{ or}\\x=π-θ+k\cdot 2π\end{cases}\) with \(k\in\mathbb{Z}.\)

The Equation \(\cos(x)=\cos(θ)\)

Recall that for two angles to have the same cosine, they must either be equal or opposite (negatives of each other).

Thus, the equation has solutions \(x=-θ\) or \(x=θ.\)

However, since adding \(k\cdot 2π\) to an angle leaves its cosine unchanged, we ultimately get:

\(\quad ! \quad \cos(x)=\cos(θ)\iff\begin{cases}x=-θ+k\cdot 2π\\\text{ or}\\x=θ+k\cdot 2π\end{cases}\) with \(k\in\mathbb{Z}.\)

The Equation \(\sec(x)=\sec(θ)\)

The equation \(\sec(x)=\sec(θ)\) can be rewritten as \(\cfrac{1}{\cos(x)}=\cfrac{1}{\cos(θ)}\iff\) \(\cos(x)=\cos(θ)\)

Therefore, it has the same solutions as \(\cos(x)=\cos(θ):\)

\(\quad ! \quad \sec(x)=\sec(θ)\iff\begin{cases}x=-θ+k\cdot 2π\\\text{ or}\\x=θ+k\cdot 2π\end{cases}\) with \(k\in\mathbb{Z}.\)

The Equation \(\tan(x)=\tan(θ)\)

From the definition of tangent on the unit circle (the tangent of an angle equals the \(y\)-coordinate of the intersection point between the line \(x=1\) and the ray defining the angle extended as a line), it follows that for two angles to have the same tangent, they must either be equal or differ by integer multiples of \(π.\)

That is:

\(\quad ! \quad \tan(x)=\tan(θ)\iff x=θ+k\cdot π\) with \(k\in\mathbb{Z}.\)

The Equation \(\cot(x)=\cot(θ)\)

The equation \(\cot(x)=\cot(θ)\) can be rewritten as \(\cfrac{1}{\tan(x)}=\cfrac{1}{\tan(θ)}\iff\) \(\tan(x)=\tan(θ)\)

Therefore, it has the same solutions as \(\tan(x)=\tan(θ):\)

\(\quad ! \quad \cot(x)=\cot(θ)\iff x=θ+k\cdot π\) with \(k\in\mathbb{Z}.\)

Examples

We will examine several cases of trigonometric equations that are not initially given in the forms above, but can be converted into them.

In all of them, we will omit \(k\in\mathbb{Z}\) at the end, but it is understood to apply.

\(\quad ! \quad \sin(x)=\cfrac{1}{2}\iff \sin(x)=\sin(\frac{π}{6})\iff \begin{cases}x=\cfrac{π}{6}+k\cdot 2π\\\text{ or}\\x=π-\cfrac{π}{6}+k\cdot 2π\end{cases}\iff \begin{cases}x=\cfrac{π}{6}+k\cdot 2π\\\text{ or}\\x=\cfrac{5π}{6}+k\cdot 2π\end{cases}\)

\(\quad ! \quad \cos(2x)=1\iff \cos(2x)=\cos(0)\iff \begin{cases}2x=0+k\cdot 2π\\\text{ or}\\2x=-0+k\cdot 2π\end{cases}\iff 2x=k\cdot 2π\iff x=k\cdot π\)

\(\quad ! \quad \sin(x)=-\cfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\iff \sin(x)=-\sin(\frac{π}{4})\iff \sin(x)=\sin(-\frac{π}{4}) \iff \begin{cases}x=-\cfrac{π}{4}+k\cdot 2π\\\text{ or}\\x=π+\cfrac{π}{4}+k\cdot 2π\end{cases}\iff \begin{cases}x=-\cfrac{π}{4}+k\cdot 2π\\\text{ or}\\x=\cfrac{5π}{4}+k\cdot 2π\end{cases}\)

\(\quad ! \quad \cos(x-\frac{π}{3})=\cfrac{1}{2}\iff \cos(x-\frac{π}{3})=\cos(\frac{π}{3})\iff \begin{cases}x-\cfrac{π}{3}=\cfrac{π}{3}+k\cdot 2π\\\text{ or}\\x-\cfrac{π}{3}=-\cfrac{π}{3}+k\cdot 2π\end{cases}\iff \begin{cases}x=\cfrac{2π}{3}+k\cdot 2π\\\text{ or}\\x=k\cdot 2π\end{cases}\)

\(\quad ! \quad 2\sin^2(x)=1\iff \sin^2(x)=\cfrac{1}{2}\iff \begin{cases}\sin(x)=-\cfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\\\text{ or}\\\sin(x)=\cfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\end{cases}\iff \begin{cases}\sin(x)=\sin(-\frac{π}{4})\\\text{ or}\\\sin(x)=\sin(\frac{π}{4})\end{cases}\iff \begin{cases}x=-\cfrac{π}{4}+k\cdot 2π\\\text{ or}\\x=\cfrac{5π}{4}+k\cdot 2π\\\text{ or}\\x=\cfrac{π}{4}+k\cdot 2π\\\text{ or}\\x=\cfrac{3π}{4}+k\cdot 2π\end{cases}\)

\(\quad ! \quad \sin(x)\cdot \cos(x)=2\,\sin(x)\iff \sin(x)\cdot \cos(x)-2\,\sin(x)=0\iff \sin(x)(\cos(x)-2)=0\iff \begin{cases}\sin(x)=0\\\text{ or}\\\cos(x)-2=0\end{cases} \iff \begin{cases}x=k\cdot π\\\text{ or}\\\cos(x)=2,\text{ impossible}\end{cases}\iff x=k\cdot π\)

\(\quad ! \quad \sin(x)=\cos(x)\iff \sin(x)=\sin(\frac{π}{2}-x)\iff \begin{cases}x=\frac{π}{2}-x+k\cdot 2π\\\text{ or}\\x=π-\cfrac{π}{2}+x+k\cdot 2π\end{cases}\iff \begin{cases}2x=\cfrac{π}{2}+k\cdot 2π\\\text{ or}\\0=\cfrac{π}{2}+k\cdot 2π,\text{ impossible}\end{cases}\iff 2x=\cfrac{π}{2}+k\cdot 2π\iff x=\cfrac{π}{4}+k\cdot π\)

\(\quad ! \quad \sin(x)-\sqrt{3}\,\cos(x)=0 \iff \sin(x)=\sqrt{3}\,\cos(x)\iff \cfrac{\sin(x)}{\cos(x)}=\sqrt{3}\iff \tan(x)=\sqrt{3}\iff \tan(x)=\tan(\frac{π}{3})\iff x=\cfrac{π}{3}+k\cdot π\)

In this example (the last one), we used the fact that it is impossible to simultaneously have \(\sin(x)=0\) and \(\cos(x)=0,\) due to the identity \(\sin^2(x)+\cos^2(x)=1\) (of course, we knew this anyway from the unit circle).

Thus, we were allowed to divide both sides of the equation by either \(\sin(x)\) or \(\cos(x),\) and we chose \(\cos(x)\) to form a tangent instead of a cotangent.

We could have done the same in the example \(\sin(x)=\cos(x),\) but there we chose to show the reduction to the first quadrant instead.

Note

If we want solutions in a specific interval, we simply find the values of \(k\in\mathbb{Z}\) for which the solutions lie within that interval.

For example, suppose we want to solve the equation \(\sin(x)=\cos(x)\) in the interval \([3π,5π].\)

We just found all solutions to this equation: \(x=\cfrac{π}{4}+k\cdot π\) with \(k\in\mathbb{Z}.\)

By inspection, we find that for \(k=3,\) we obtain the solution \(x=\cfrac{π}{4}+3π=\cfrac{13π}{4},\) which lies in \([3π,5π].\)

To find the rest, we simply check adjacent values of \(k:\)

  • For \(k=2,\) we obtain the solution \(x=\cfrac{π}{4}+2π,\) which does not belong to \([3π,5π].\) Thus, we do not need to check smaller values of \(k.\)
  • For \(k=4,\) we obtain the solution \(x=\cfrac{π}{4}+4π=\cfrac{17π}{4},\) which belongs to \([3π,5π].\)
  • For \(k=5,\) we obtain the solution \(x=\cfrac{π}{4}+5π,\) which does not belong to \([3π,5π].\) Thus, we do not need to check larger values of \(k.\)

Therefore, the solutions of the equation \(\sin(x)=\cos(x)\) in the interval \([3π,5π]\) are \(x=\cfrac{13π}{4}\) and \(x=\cfrac{17π}{4}.\)

Useful Formulas

We will examine various formulas used either in proof exercises (e.g., to prove an identity containing trigonometric values) or to compute the trigonometric values of non-standard angles.

Trigonometric Values Of Sum And Difference Of Angles

For any \(a,b\in\mathbb{R},\) the following hold:

  • \(\sin(a+b)=\) \(\sin(a)\cdot \cos(b)+\cos(a)\cdot \sin(b)\)
  • \(\sin(a-b)=\) \(\sin(a)\cdot \cos(b)-\cos(a)\cdot \sin(b)\)
  • \(\cos(a+b)=\) \(\cos(a)\cdot \cos(b)-\sin(a)\cdot \sin(b)\)
  • \(\cos(a-b)=\) \(\cos(a)\cdot \cos(b)+\sin(a)\cdot \sin(b)\)
  • \(\tan(a+b)=\) \(\cfrac{\tan(a)+\tan(b)}{1-\tan(a)\cdot \tan(b)}\)
  • \(\tan(a-b)=\) \(\cfrac{\tan(a)-\tan(b)}{1+\tan(a)\cdot \tan(b)}\)

Note

The formulas for tangent hold whenever the denominators are non-zero.

Example

Using these formulas, we can compute the trigonometric values of the angle \(\cfrac{π}{12}\) \((15^{\circ}),\) which is not a standard angle.

Specifically:

\(\quad ! \quad \sin(\frac{π}{12})=\sin(\frac{π}{3}-\frac{π}{4})=\sin(\frac{π}{3})\cdot \cos(\frac{π}{4})-\cos(\frac{π}{3})\cdot \sin(\frac{π}{4})=\cfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\cdot\cfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}-\cfrac{1}{2}\cdot\cfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}=\cfrac{\sqrt{6}-\sqrt{2}}{4}\)

Similarly for \(\cos(\frac{π}{12})\) and \(\tan(\frac{π}{12}).\)

Trigonometric Values Of Double Angles

For any \(a\in\mathbb{R},\) the following hold:

  • \(\sin(2a)=2\cdot \sin(a)\cdot \cos(a)\)
  • \(\sin(a)\cdot \cos(a)=\cfrac{\sin(2a)}{2}\)
  • \(\cos(2a)=\cos^2(a)-\sin^2(a)\)
  • \(\cos(2a)=2\cos^2(a)-1\)
  • \(\cos(2a)=1-2\sin^2(a)\)
  • \(\tan(2a)=\cfrac{2\cdot \tan(a)}{1-\tan^2(a)}\)

Note

The formula for tangent hold whenever the denominator is non-zero.

Trigonometric Values Of Triple Angles

For any \(a\in\mathbb{R},\) the following hold:

  • \(\sin(3a)=3\sin(a)-4\sin^3(a)\)
  • \(\sin(3a)=\) \(3\sin(a)\cos^2(a)-\sin^3(a)\)
  • \(\cos(3a)=4\cos^3(a)-3\cos(a)\)
  • \(\cos(3a)=\) \(\cos^3(a)-3\cos(a)\sin^2(a)\)
  • \(\tan(3a)=\cfrac{3\tan(a)-\tan^3(a)}{1-3\tan^2(a)}\)

Note

The formula for tangent holds whenever the denominator is non-zero.

Trigonometric Values Of Half Angles

For any \(a\in\mathbb{R},\) the following hold:

  • \(\sin^2\!\left(\frac{a}{2}\right)=\cfrac{1-\cos(a)}{2}\)
  • \(\cos^2\!\left(\frac{a}{2}\right)=\cfrac{1+\cos(a)}{2}\)
  • \(\tan\!\left(\frac{a}{2}\right)=\cfrac{\sin(a)}{1+\cos(a)}\)
  • \(\tan\!\left(\frac{a}{2}\right)=\cfrac{1-\cos(a)}{\sin(a)}\)

Note

The formulas for tangent hold whenever the denominators are non-zero.

Product-To-Sum Formulas

For any \(a,b\in\mathbb{R},\) the following hold:

  • \(\sin(a)\sin(b)=\) \(\cfrac{\cos(a-b)-\cos(a+b)}{2}\)
  • \(\cos(a)\cos(b)=\) \(\cfrac{\cos(a-b)+\cos(a+b)}{2}\)
  • \(\sin(a)\cos(b)=\) \(\cfrac{\sin(a+b)+\sin(a-b)}{2}\)
  • \(\cos(a)\sin(b)=\) \(\cfrac{\sin(a+b)-\sin(a-b)}{2}\)

Sum-To-Product Formulas

For any \(a,b\in\mathbb{R},\) the following hold:

  • \(\sin(a)+\sin(b)=\) \(2\sin\!\left(\frac{a+b}{2}\right)\cos\!\left(\frac{a-b}{2}\right)\)
  • \(\sin(a)-\sin(b)=\) \(2\cos\!\left(\frac{a+b}{2}\right)\sin\!\left(\frac{a-b}{2}\right)\)
  • \(\cos(a)+\cos(b)=\) \(2\cos\!\left(\frac{a+b}{2}\right)\cos\!\left(\frac{a-b}{2}\right)\)
  • \(\cos(a)-\cos(b)=\) \(-2\sin\!\left(\frac{a+b}{2}\right)\sin\!\left(\frac{a-b}{2}\right)\)
